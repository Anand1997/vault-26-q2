# Week 1 
 ## **Phase 1: Defining the End State (Endgames)**
In software, you often design your API contracts and expected outputs before writing the internal logic. Chess is the same.
- IM Daniel Rensch recommends a "Backwards-to-Forwards" approach to the game.
- Capablanca’s _Chess Fundamentals_ emphasizes starting with elementary mates to learn proper piece coordination.
- Before playing full games, you need to master basic checkmates, specifically the King & Queen vs. King and the Two Rooks vs. King.
- **Actionable Task:** Spend 15 minutes daily practicing these basic checkmates against a computer until the pattern feels like muscle memory. Think of this as passing your foundational unit tests.

## **Phase 2: Core Processing Logic (Middlegames)**
Once you know how the program should terminate, you can optimize the middle logic.
- The middlegame is dynamic and requires continuous evaluation, such as assessing your king's safety and your overall piece activity.
- Active pieces are the core engine of a successful plan; they should work together to control the center and attack opponent weaknesses.
- **Actionable Task:** When playing, ensure every piece is "doing work" (avoiding idle threads) and never leave your King exposed in the center.

## **Phase 3: System Initialization (Openings)**
We want to avoid "dependency bloat." You do not need to memorize deep, complex opening theory.
- Focus on general principles: controlling the center and developing active pieces carefully.
- The Italian Game is a perfect, principled framework to adopt, as it aims to control the center quickly with a pawn and knight while preparing to castle to safety.
- **Actionable Task:** Review the first three moves of the Italian Game (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4) to set up a safe, active foundation for your pieces.

## **Therapist Reset (ADHD Strategy):**

Chess requires sustained mental load. If you find your focus drifting or you start impulsively moving pieces without a plan, step away from the board for a moment. Take a breath, look at the physical edges of your screen, and manually recount the material on the board (count your pawns, knights, and bishops). This manual, low-level task will actively pull your brain back into the present state of the game and reset your working memory.
